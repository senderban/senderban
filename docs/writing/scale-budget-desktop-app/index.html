A scale budget for a desktop app: 50 accounts, 5 slots, a deadline on every job

Scale budgets are usually written for servers. A tray app that holds up to 50 mail accounts can still storm a provider with logins and freeze its own window. Here is the budget, how it found its way into the code, and what it costs.

The unit is the account

A scale budget answers four questions: the unit, what grows linearly with it, the number at 10 and 50 units, and the share each unit gets of a shared resource. The app's budget is ADR 0006, and later decisions that add work, such as finding a provider from an address, carry their own budget section. Here the unit is the account, capped at MAX_ACCOUNTS = 50. Nothing is linear in mail volume: a new mail is one fetch of its sender and subject and one notification, never a sync.

Each number in the budget carries its basis: measured in the repository, derived from constants in the code, or unmeasured. None of them is a provider's limit, because no provider's login throttle has been measured.

The storm a review found

One persistent IMAP connection per account is fine at 1 account and fine at 50. The problem is the moment they all start together: at launch, after the laptop wakes up, or when the person presses Resume. Without a limit, 50 accounts open 50 logins at the same instant. A block clicked in a notification did the same: it wrote the rule to every account at once, 50 provider rounds in parallel.

The answer is one shared gate per resource, not one per account: a first-in, first-out semaphore, createGate(max). All watchers share one gate for logins (MAX_CONCURRENT_LOGINS = 5), the sync engine owns the gates for provider rounds (MAX_CONCURRENT_SYNCS = 5 on the main one). The test perf/storm.test.ts keeps both: a baseline without the gate opens every login at once, and with the gate the count never passes 5 and every account still connects. Resume needs no extra jitter: fifty reconnects queue behind the gate and start five at a time.

A gate needs a deadline

A gate turns one stuck account into everyone's problem: a login that never ends holds a slot for good. So every gated job has a deadline. A login is abandoned after CONNECT_SLOT_MS, 60 seconds; the deadline frees the slot and aborts a signal, and the IMAP connector closes its socket at once. The pre-push performance review measured why the abort matters: without it, a provider-wide stall held 25 sockets, five times the cap.

One part is honest about its gap. A ManageSieve connect() is not cancelled; it ends through its own 15-second steps, so a hung Sieve login can outlive its slot by up to 30 seconds. In simulation that peaked at 10 sockets, twice the cap. It is written down as such.

The numbers at 1, 10 and 50 accounts

Accounts 1 10 50
Persistent IMAP sockets (derived) 1 10 50
IMAP logins in flight after start or resume 1 5 5 (was 50)
ManageSieve commands per block (measured) 10 64 304
Time to open every login, 2 s each (derived) 2 s 4 s about 20 s

With every account kind together (15 Sieve, 10 Gmail, 10 Microsoft and 15 IMAP cleaning at 50 accounts), one block click measured 6 to 8 seconds of main-thread CPU in total, and the longest stretch without an event-loop turn was 1.0 second. That was the real engine and store with fake providers on a loaded laptop, not a real provider's number.

What it costs

  • At 50 accounts an Unblock takes about 40 seconds instead of about 4, because its two rounds per account queue behind 5 slots. Up to about 3 accounts nothing changes. A reserved slot for clicks is the fix if a pilot shows it matters; it is not built.
  • A block clicked while a wave of rounds is draining waits behind it, about 20 seconds at 50 accounts. Background lanes such as Gmail and the MXroute blacklist have their own gate, so they cannot take the slots a click waits for.
  • 5 is a starting value, not a measured limit: two named constants, to be tuned once a provider's login throttle is measured.
  • Not measured yet: 50 real connections at once, keepalive on the wire, sleep and resume on a real laptop, and the providers' own throttles.

The general lesson: on a desktop, the shared resource is the person's provider and the person's own window. Linear in accounts is acceptable; all at once is not, and every slot needs a way to be given back.
